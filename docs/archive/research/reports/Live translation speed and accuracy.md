# Serial chunking keeps Baihe's Live captions far behind

Baihe's Live captions are not close to real-time yet. With default settings, a word spoken in the middle of a stream appears roughly **18 to 19 seconds** after Baihe receives it, and up to about 30 seconds in the worst case. That figure comes before translation time and before the delay the streaming site adds itself. It is worked out from the code's own settings and the repo's own CPU speed test, not timed on a real stream. The fastest shipped products publish about **2 to 3 seconds** of lag (Google Meet about 2 s, Meta's glasses 2.7 s). Those are vendor claims, and neither covers Chinese, Japanese or Korean. Apple publishes no figure for AirPods Live Translation, and no reviewer has timed it. Reviewers say it waits until near the end of a sentence before translating. (I found no sign of Apple running it at airports, so "airport" almost certainly meant AirPods.) Most of Baihe's lag is structural and fixable. Live cuts audio into 20-second slices, runs speech recognition on a slice only once it is complete, and then translates each caption with its own model call on the same thread. While that translation runs, the next slice waits. Baihe already contains a faster, word-by-word recognition component, but nothing uses it, even though `docs/STATUS.md` says Live does. Live also uses a weaker speech model than the one the repo's own tests favour, and it sends the translator none of the glossary, character names or locale that bulk translation uses. A handful of small changes would cut several seconds and improve names. Getting to the 3 to 5 seconds that research systems manage for these languages needs the larger streaming rework. The highly used bulk features have narrower, cheaper wins, plus one ready-made caching fix sitting on an unmerged branch.

**How to read the evidence in this report.** Three labels mark every important number. **[measured]** means someone ran it and reported the result: the repo's own tests, or a third party. **[vendor claim]** means the maker of a product or model said it about their own work. **[inference]** means a figure worked out from code constants or other numbers, which nobody has timed. The repository analysis was made at commit `8231d96d`. I re-checked the key constants (model list, 2 s poll, 15 s video delay, 3-segment edge join, per-caption translation call, unused agreement module) on the current branch head `2c9127af`, and they are unchanged.

## AirPods wait for sentences; the 2-second products skip Asian languages

"Real-time" in shipped products means different things. Apple's Live Translation runs on the iPhone with AirPods Pro 2/3, AirPods 4 with noise cancellation and newer models. It covers Mandarin (Simplified and Traditional), Japanese and Korean among its languages. Apple says it processes speech on-device with generative models and warns that "translations may be inaccurate" ([Apple Newsroom](https://www.apple.com/ie/newsroom/2025/11/live-translation-on-airpods-expands-to-the-eu/); [Live From A Lounge](https://livefromalounge.com/apple-live-translation-gets-more-useful-for-travellers-with-ios-27/)). Neither Apple nor any reviewer I found publishes a delay in seconds. Macworld found the AirPods "often waited until near the end of a sentence before translating", with "substantial" delays on long German clauses ([Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/3012435/airpods-live-translation-english-german-iphone-testing.html)). Which?'s testers ended up reading the on-screen text because it beat the spoken audio ([Which?](https://www.which.co.uk/news/article/we-tested-apples-live-translation-feature-does-it-really-work-aClEt6o0zOut)). A New York Times test in Japan found it "almost effortless" one-on-one in quiet places. It struggled in stations, bars and with fast talkers, and it got some sushi terms and pronouns wrong ([The Nightly / NYT](https://thenightly.com.au/lifestyle/travel/we-tested-apples-airpod-translation-in-tokyo-heres-what-we-found-c-21147816)). That behaviour suggests a **cascade**: separate speech-recognition, translation and speech-synthesis steps that wait for a sentence boundary. This is **[inference]**, because Apple has not published its design.

The products that publish numbers look like this:

| System | Published lag | Evidence | Covers zh/ja/ko? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Google Meet speech-to-speech model (Nov 2025) | about 2 s, versus 4-5 s for its older cascade | [vendor claim] ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/real-time-speech-to-speech-translation/)) | No (en with es, de, fr, it, pt) |
| Meta Ray-Ban glasses, fully on-device | 2.7 s, down from over 5 s | [vendor claim] ([Meta](https://www.meta.com/blog/live-translation-ai-glasses-deep-dive/)) | No |
| Apple AirPods Live Translation | none published; "waits near sentence end" | independent but untimed | Yes |
| Gemini 3.5 Live Translate, Microsoft Teams Interpreter, DeepL Voice, Samsung | "low" or "minimal" latency, no figures | [vendor claim] | Teams, DeepL and Samsung yes; Gemini unconfirmed |
| Research systems, English to Chinese / Japanese (IWSLT 2025) | 3.7 s / 4.65 s | [measured] by authors, simulated, compute time excluded ([CUNI](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.17077v1)) | Opposite direction |
| Baihe Live, default settings | about 18-19 s typical, about 30 s worst, plus translation and site delay | [inference] from code and repo CPU benchmark | Yes |

Google explains why the fast products skip Asian languages. Its model translates with a fixed **lookahead**: it always listens a set amount ahead, 2 s for conversation. Google names languages with very different word order as the case where a fixed lookahead fails ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/real-time-speech-to-speech-translation/)). Japanese and Korean put the verb at the end of the sentence, so an English translation often cannot start until the sentence is nearly over. In practice, a realistic target for Japanese, Korean or Chinese into English captions is **about 2 to 5 seconds**. The low end matches the best products. The high end matches the research systems for these languages. This range is **[inference]**. Baihe also has two structural advantages. Captions skip the speech-synthesis step, which reviewers found slower than text. For recorded video, Baihe can use the whole episode as context, which no live product can.

## Baihe waits for 20-second slices, then does everything in single file

Live works as one background job ([`live_translate.py`](../live_translate.py)). yt-dlp finds the stream. Baihe's own fetcher pulls the site's **HLS** playlist (the way most live sites deliver video, as a rolling list of few-second files) and joins **3 segments** behind the newest one ([`live_fetch.py`](../live_fetch.py):49). ffmpeg then cuts the audio into fixed **chunks** (slices of audio handled as one unit). They are **20 s** by default, or 4 s with the "Fast captions" preset ([`frontend/src/api/live.ts`](../frontend/src/api/live.ts)). Each chunk is processed only after the next one has started, which proves the file is finished. The loop checks for new chunks every **2 seconds** ([`live_translate.py`](../live_translate.py):557).

Each chunk goes to faster-whisper (the Whisper speech-recognition model) with the last 3 s of the previous chunk re-heard as an **overlap**. **VAD** (voice activity detection, a small model that separates speech from silence and music so Whisper only hears speech) is on. The previous chunk's last 200 characters of text are passed as a hint. Then every caption from that chunk is translated **one at a time, one model call each**, on the same thread ([`live_cue_feed.py`](../live_cue_feed.py):50; [`live_cue_translation.py`](../live_cue_translation.py):54). The next chunk's speech recognition cannot start until all of those translations finish. If more than 2 finished chunks pile up, Baihe deletes the older ones unheard and shows "Skipped N chunks to catch up" ([`live_translate.py`](../live_translate.py):421). Captions never appear partway through a chunk, and they are never revised once shown.

The only speech-recognition speed data in the repo is **[measured]** on a 4-core CPU. Whisper `small` took 92 s for the first 300 s of a Chinese streamer recording, model loading included. `large-v3-turbo` took 145 s and `medium` 236 s ([`docs/asr-experiments.md`](../docs/asr-experiments.md):329-362). The repo has no GPU timings at all. Applied to Live's settings, those numbers give this budget, which is **[inference]** throughout:

| Where the word falls in its 20 s chunk | Waiting for chunk to close | Loop check | Whisper on 23 s of audio (CPU, small) | Delivery to browser | Total before translation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| End (best case) | about 0 s | 0 s | about 7 s | under 1 s | about 7-8 s |
| Middle (typical) | 10 s | about 1 s | about 7 s | under 1 s | about 18-19 s |
| Start (worst case) | 20 s | 2 s | about 7 s | under 1 s | about 29-30 s |

Three things come on top of these totals. First, the translation time for that caption and every earlier caption in the same chunk. Second, the HLS join, 3 to 4 segment lengths behind the live edge, which is site-dependent and not recorded in the repo. Third, the site's own broadcast delay. The biggest contributors, in order, are the chunk length, Whisper's time per chunk, the serial per-caption translation that also blocks the next Whisper call, the 3-segment HLS join, and the 2 s loop check.

Two shortcuts look tempting and do less than they seem. Fast captions (4 s chunks) cut the average wait for a chunk to close to about 2 s. But Whisper pads every call to a 30-second window internally, so a 5-second call probably costs a large share of what a 23-second call costs. On a CPU, Fast captions may fall behind real time and start dropping audio. That is **[inference]** and the first thing to measure. Second, the video-delay slider defaults to **15 s** ([`frontend/src/pages/live/embedLogic.ts`](../frontend/src/pages/live/embedLogic.ts):13). That is shorter than the typical 18 s+ lag, so most captions land late against the delayed picture.

The code review also found three defects. **The word-by-word streaming component is written but unwired.** `StreamAgreement` in [`live_agreement.py`](../live_agreement.py) is used only by its own tests, and the claim in [`docs/STATUS.md`](../docs/STATUS.md):15 that Live uses "LocalAgreement-2 streaming recognition" is stale. **The last chunk of a stream is never transcribed.** It has no successor file, and the loop exits when ffmpeg stops. **The live translator never gets the glossary.** A docstring says `build_translation_context` is shared by Live and bulk translation, but Live has its own, thinner builder ([`live_cue_translation.py`](../live_cue_translation.py)).

## Accuracy suffers from a small model, no names and per-slice filters

The single largest accuracy lever is one the repo has already measured. On the streamer recording, `small`, Live's default, scored a **CER of 23.4**. CER is character error rate, the share of characters wrong. `large-v3-turbo` scored **16.4**, and `medium` 17.5 while running slower than turbo **[measured]** ([`docs/asr-experiments.md`](../docs/asr-experiments.md):346-362). Live offers only tiny, base, small and medium ([`services/live_service.py`](../services/live_service.py):53), even though batch transcription defaults to turbo. Outside the repo, a third-party Japanese benchmark on clean read speech found turbo within about 0.3 CER points of large-v3 at roughly 1.6 times the speed **[measured]** ([Classmethod](https://dev.classmethod.jp/articles/japanese-asr-l40s-fleurs-benchmark/)).

The second lever is names. Live sends the translator a fixed 1.2k-character instruction block, the last 4 caption pairs and the new caption. It sends no glossary, character names, style guide, language pack or locale, so every session asks for "American English" regardless of the user's setting. Whisper gets no name hint either. In batch transcription, by contrast, the series glossary names go into Whisper's prompt ([`services/transcribe_service.py`](../services/transcribe_service.py):228-261). A name not said in the last four captions has nothing anchoring its spelling. The research backs prioritising this. Google's TranslateGemma human evaluation found its Japanese-to-English quality fell below the base model, and traced the drop to **named-entity mistranslation** **[measured]** ([TranslateGemma report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.09012)). Studies also find that extra document context improves LLM translation in general but does not reliably fix pronouns ([Mohammed and Niculae](https://dare.uva.nl/id/b11cc1d6-8078-47cf-919d-5d5bfae872ec)). Explicit names and speaker hints beat simply sending more preceding lines. The owner has already decided (2026-10-10) that Live should get an optional title picker. Nothing implements it yet.

The third lever is non-speech audio. Whisper large-v3 produces text for **40.3% of non-speech inputs**, typically subtitle credits, thank-yous and applause markers **[measured]** ([Barański et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.11378)). Live has VAD on and drops runs of four or more identical segments. But that filter runs inside one chunk at a time, so a phrase hallucinated once per 20-second chunk over background music is never a run of four and passes through. Worse, the emitted text becomes the next chunk's hint, so a hallucination can seed the next one. That is **[inference]**; the code has no guard. VAD alone does not catch speech-like music. SenseVoiceSmall tags background music as an audio event, which could serve as a second gate **[vendor claim]** ([SenseVoiceSmall](https://huggingface.co/FunAudioLLM/SenseVoiceSmall)).

## What 2026 projects do: commit early, translate committed lines, never flicker

The open-source state of the art has moved from "wait for a slice" to **streaming policies**: rules that decide which words are safe to show while audio is still arriving. **LocalAgreement** commits a word only once two consecutive guesses on growing audio agree. Its original implementation, whisper_streaming, reports about **3.3 s** latency **[vendor claim]**, with no hardware stated ([whisper_streaming](https://github.com/ufal/whisper_streaming)). The same authors now steer users to **SimulStreaming**, which uses **AlignAtt**: it watches where the model is "looking" in the audio and stops before words at the very edge. They describe it as "~5-times faster" and the best system at IWSLT 2025. They also say it needs a GPU with 10 GB or more, because CPU is "too slow for real time" **[vendor claim]** ([SimulStreaming](https://github.com/ufal/SimulStreaming)). **WhisperLiveKit** (Apache-2.0, about 11.1k stars) wraps SimulStreaming behind a WebSocket server. It adds Chinese-, Japanese- and Korean-capable backends (SenseVoice, Qwen3-ASR, Voxtral), a distilled NLLB translator and a Chrome tab-audio extension. It keeps VAD on by default ("disabling NOT ADVISED") and publishes no latency numbers in text ([WhisperLiveKit](https://github.com/QuentinFuxa/WhisperLiveKit)).

The other shared idea is to separate **partial** text from **committed** text, to avoid **flicker**: on-screen words that keep changing as the system revises its guess. Apple's own speech framework emits rough "volatile" text at once and replaces it with a final result ([Apple WWDC25](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/277/)). WhisperLive has a separate "committed transcript" callback ([WhisperLive](https://github.com/collabora/WhisperLive)). Users of LiveCaptions-Translator have an open request (#278) for a stable "Reading Mode" because captions jump around ([issues](https://github.com/SakiRinn/LiveCaptions-Translator/issues)). Google's research showed that hiding the last few still-changing words and nudging each new guess to match the previous one "nearly eliminates" flicker **[vendor claim]** ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/stabilizing-live-speech-translation-in-google-translate/)). For Baihe, which already never revises a caption, the pattern is: show the source transcript dimmed as soon as it exists, translate only committed lines, and never rewrite a translation on screen. Baihe's "transcript first" display is half of this already.

Newer speech models also challenge Whisper for these languages. Only three open models cover Chinese, Japanese and Korean in one model with a streaming path. **Qwen3-ASR** (Apache-2.0, 0.6B/1.7B) reports a Chinese error rate of 2.41 on FLEURS, with streaming slightly worse than offline (average 3.33 vs 2.69) **[vendor claim]**. It publishes no Japanese or Korean figures in text ([Qwen3-ASR card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-ASR-1.7B)). **Voxtral Mini 4B Realtime** has an adjustable delay. At 2.4 s it reports Japanese CER 5.50 and Chinese CER 8.48, against offline Whisper's 4.97 and 7.94 in the same paper **[vendor claim]** ([Voxtral Realtime](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11298v2)). **SenseVoiceSmall** claims 70 ms for 10 s of audio but is not natively streaming **[vendor claim]**. Every number here is on clean read speech; none is on drama audio with music. Korean is weakest across the board: about 14% word error even for Whisper on FLEURS ([Voxtral Realtime](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11298v2)). On the translation side, purpose-built 7B models rival frontier ones on automatic scores. Hunyuan-MT-7B scores 0.90 XCOMET into English against 0.94 for Gemini 2.5 Pro, and is rated close to it by humans on Chinese-English **[vendor claim]** ([Hunyuan-MT report](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.05209v2)). TranslateGemma's Japanese name problem argues for keeping glossary injection whichever model is used.

Speed on the translation side is not the bottleneck it looks like, provided calls are batched. A 7-8B model at 4-bit generates about **77 tokens/s on an RTX 3060** and **189 tokens/s on an RTX 4090** **[measured]**, by community benchmark ([llama.cpp #15013](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/15013)). That is enough for a chunk's worth of short captions in a second or two **[inference]**. The cost is in Baihe's call pattern: one call per caption, each re-reading the same instructions. Live also asks Ollama for a 16k-token context window ([`engine_backends/local.py`](../engine_backends/local.py):28) when the prompt is under 2k characters. On a shared GPU, that wastes memory that Whisper needs **[inference]**.

## Ranked recommendations for Live: thread the pipeline first, rebuild streaming last

The table is ordered by expected gain per unit of effort and risk. Size is S (a day or less), M (a few days) or L (a multi-PR project). "Measure first" names the number that should exist before or alongside the change. Most of these come from Baihe's existing per-chunk log line (`log_chunk` in [`live_whisper.py`](../live_whisper.py):453-481). It already records audio length, queue wait, Whisper seconds and translate seconds.

| Rank | Change | Expected effect | Size | Risk | Measure first |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Run translation on its own worker thread so Whisper never waits for the LLM | Each chunk needs max(Whisper, translate) seconds instead of their sum. Fewer dropped chunks, and the next chunk starts on time [inference] | M | Medium: Stop/cancel, per-thread context vars, ordering | `translate` seconds as a share of chunk length; count of "Skipped N chunks" notes |
| 2 | Offer `large-v3-turbo` in Live; demote `medium` | CER 23.4 to 16.4 on the repo's streamer test [measured], at about 1.6x the CPU time of small [measured] | S | Low-medium: more drops on weak CPUs | Whisper seconds per chunk, small vs turbo, on the owner's machine (CPU and GPU) |
| 3 | Three quick lag trims: loop check 2 s to 0.25-0.5 s; default video delay follows chunk length plus measured processing time; process the final chunk(s) when a stream ends | About 1 s off the average, captions land on the right picture, the last 20 s+ of a stream is no longer lost [inference] | S each | Low | Queue wait in `log_chunk`; share of captions shown at arrival rather than on schedule |
| 4 | Translate each chunk's captions in one id-keyed call (keep per-caption calls for 1-2 caption chunks) | N model round trips become one. Lower cost and translate time [inference] | S-M | Low-medium; matching by id already exists | Captions per chunk (add the count to `log_chunk`); translate seconds before and after |
| 5 | Title picker: glossary, character names, language pack and locale into the translator, glossary names into Whisper's hint (owner decision) | Consistent name spelling and translation; correct locale [inference, supported by TranslateGemma's name finding] | M | Medium: ownership checks, prompt growth, CLI parity note | Name-spelling variants per session on a stream with a known cast |
| 6 | Cross-chunk hallucination guard; don't carry a flagged line into the next hint | Fewer repeated credit and thank-you lines over music [inference] | S | Low-medium: real repeated greetings | Identical consecutive captions across chunks in a music-heavy session |
| 7 | Smaller Ollama context window for Live, sized from the prompt | Frees GPU memory for Whisper when both share a card [inference, unproven] | S-M | Medium: Ollama reloads if a bulk job uses 16k | `ollama ps` CPU/GPU split and free VRAM every chunk during a GPU session |
| 8 | Join HLS 2 segments from the edge instead of 3 | One segment length less lag [inference] | S | Medium: more stalls (ffmpeg uses 3 for a reason) | Parse `PROGRAM-DATE-TIME` from the playlist and log true distance from live |
| 9 | Low-latency mode, GPU only: wire `StreamAgreement` (LocalAgreement) with a rolling audio buffer, show dimmed partial transcript, translate committed lines; or adopt SimulStreaming/WhisperLiveKit | From about 18 s to the 3-5 s range the research systems reach [inference from vendor/author figures] | L | High: threading, repeated decoding, GPU slot | Whisper wall time for 2-15 s windows on the owner's GPU, which decides feasibility |
| 10 | Bake-off of Chinese-, Japanese- and Korean-capable speech models (turbo, Qwen3-ASR, SenseVoice, Voxtral Realtime) and translators (current Gemma, Hunyuan-MT-7B, TranslateGemma) on the owner's own drama and stream clips | Picks the real winner for this audio. All public numbers are on clean read speech | M-L | Low (evaluation only), but adds optional dependencies if adopted | CER and a small human or chrF check per language on 10-20 minutes of real clips |
| 11 | Beam 1 for Fast captions only | Faster decoding, at a cost of 1.9 CER points on the streamer test [measured] | S | Medium (accuracy) | Whisper seconds at 4 s chunks vs 20 s chunks first, since padding may erase the gain |

The first eight items are each a day or a few days and together should take the typical lag down by several seconds and stop dropped audio. The bigger remaining lag, the 10-second average wait for a 20-second chunk to close, only goes away with item 9. Before starting item 9, one instrumented run should settle four unknowns: Whisper seconds per chunk at 4 s and 20 s chunks, translate seconds per caption and per chunk, captions per chunk, and free GPU memory with Ollama loaded. Use a 10-minute HLS stream per setting, across chunk size, small vs turbo, CPU vs GPU, and local vs hosted translator, and record from `log_chunk`. Add timestamps for when each segment arrives, when each chunk file closes and when each caption is published. The rules then fall out of the data. If translate time is a large share of the chunk, do items 1 and 4 first. If a 4-second Whisper call costs nearly as much as a 20-second one, stop offering Fast captions on CPU and make item 9 GPU-only.

## Bulk transcription and translation: one unmerged fix and three cheap upgrades

The everyday flows are in better shape than Live. Bulk translation already sends id-keyed batches of 30 lines with 10 lines of context behind and 6 ahead, starts batches at scene breaks, and keeps a stable, cacheable instruction block ([`services/translate_run_service.py`](../services/translate_run_service.py):88-102; [`engine_backends/prompts.py`](../engine_backends/prompts.py)). That matches what llm-subtrans, the most used open-source subtitle translator, does. llm-subtrans warns that larger batches are cheaper but "desync" more often, meaning the model merges or drops lines and forces retries ([llm-subtrans](https://github.com/machinewrapped/llm-subtrans)). Baihe's id matching already avoids that failure. The cheapest win is to **merge commit `efc62304`**, which sits only on the branch `fix-caps-privacy-glossary`. It moves Claude's cache marker so that runs with a long novel reference stop paying a cache write per batch and never reading it back. It is size S and low risk, and it affects only Claude runs with a novel excerpt. Anthropic's current docs price cache reads at 0.05 to 0.1 of normal input, and the Batch API halves prices again for jobs that can wait up to 24 hours **[vendor claim]** ([Anthropic prompt caching](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching)). An opt-in "cheap, overnight" mode is therefore a cost lever, not a speed one.

For transcription, the obvious speed lever is faster-whisper's batched pipeline. It went from 63 s to 17 s on 13 minutes of audio on an RTX 3070 Ti **[vendor claim]** ([faster-whisper](https://github.com/SYSTRAN/faster-whisper)). The repo's own test argues against it on stream audio: batched "fast mode" scored CER 24.0 with 44 lines versus 16.4 with 150 lines **[measured]** ([`docs/asr-experiments.md`](../docs/asr-experiments.md):357). Beam 1 saved 7-35% of time on clean read speech with little accuracy cost **[measured]** (same doc, line 138). Keep the current defaults, and offer the faster options per title only after measuring them on that title's audio, which Baihe's auto-tune already does for silence settings. Three accuracy upgrades have outside support. First, an auto-growing terminology map that carries names found in early batches into later ones, as llm-subtrans does. Second, a forced aligner for word timing: Qwen3-ForcedAligner reports 43 ms average timing error against 133 ms for WhisperX, covering Chinese, Japanese and Korean **[vendor claim]** ([Qwen3-ASR](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-ASR-1.7B)). Third, re-translating only flagged lines with a stronger model and keeping the better version. At WMT25, choosing the best of several translations by a quality-estimation score raised COMET by about 0.02 in every language tested, while asking a model to patch flagged spans made every language worse **[measured]** by the authors ([arXiv 2511.13884](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.13884v1)). Baihe's "stronger engine" flow is the natural home for that select-don't-patch rule. Subtitle export was skimmed, not profiled, and showed no obvious hot spot.

## The missing numbers matter more than the missing features

The gaps are large. Nobody has measured live Chinese, Japanese or Korean into English on a consumer GPU: not Baihe, not the open-source projects, and not the research papers. Their latency figures exclude compute time, or run on A40, L40S or H100 datacentre cards, and cover English into Asian languages, not the reverse. No independent reviewer has timed Apple's feature or any competitor's with instruments. Every competitor figure in this report is either a vendor claim or a reviewer's impression. Baihe's own repo has no GPU speed figures, no per-chunk log from a real Live session, no measurement of how many captions a chunk produces, and no measurement of how accurate chunked Live recognition is compared with whole-file recognition. The headline claim that Baihe trails by about 18 seconds is therefore a well-founded estimate from code and a CPU benchmark, not a stopwatch reading. It could be better on a fast GPU or worse with a slow local translator. All the new speech models' accuracy figures come from clean read speech, not drama audio with music.

## Conclusion

The question "is Baihe close to AirPods?" turns out to be the wrong comparison. Apple, by reviewers' accounts, also waits for whole sentences, and the products fast enough to call real-time do not handle Baihe's languages at all. The useful benchmark is the 3 to 5 seconds that research cascades reach for these languages. Baihe is about four times slower than that, almost entirely because of how its stages are arranged, not because its models are too slow. That makes the next step cheap: an instrumented run and a threaded translation queue come before any new model. The streaming rework is the only route to single-digit lag, and the half-built agreement module means part of it is already paid for.

The more durable advantage lies elsewhere. Every live product makes the same name and pronoun mistakes, because it sees one sentence at a time. Baihe's library already holds what fixes them: series glossaries, character lists and whole-episode context. Wiring that into Live via the title picker, and keeping it central in bulk translation, is where Baihe can beat the commercial products outright rather than chase them.
