# Fix the flags first, then test the models

Baihe already follows most of 2026 best practice for CJK subtitle ASR. It uses Silero VAD, forces the source language, turns off `condition_on_previous_text`, filters stock credit phrases, uses Qwen3-ASR with Qwen3-ForcedAligner, sends id-keyed translation batches with scene-aware context and a glossary, and leaves vocal separation and denoising off by default. Where it falls short of the field is in what happens **after** recognition. Its English reading-speed flag is set at 8.5 characters per second, against Netflix's 20 for adult English, so in an offline run it flagged **every eligible line in a sample of natural dialogue**. Fix flagged then spends one paid call per flagged line and clears flags it never addressed. Nothing enforces a minimum duration, a maximum duration or an inter-cue gap. The zh/ja default backend (`qwen3_asr_long`) produces lines up to 16 s, stores no word timings, and on the repo's one real-audio test scored worse than Whisper turbo (22.0 vs 16.4 CER), even though the vendor reports Qwen3-ASR roughly halving Whisper's error on conversational Chinese. The highest-payoff work is therefore cheap and mostly testable offline: recalibrate and de-duplicate the flags, make Fix flagged reason-aware, add Netflix-style timing rules, and close the translation merge/shift hole. The model-choice questions (Qwen vs Whisper for zh/ja, large-v3 vs turbo, fast mode) need a verbatim-reference test on the owner's GPU before any default changes. Among the paid AI features, Re-segment's "Use AI" costs well under a cent to a few cents per hour and is safe by construction. The money goes to Reflect (~$1.04/hour uncached), Fix flagged (~$0.74/hour at 20% flagged) and the Flag pass, and the last two return little in their current form.

*Scope and freshness.* The two Baihe audits were run at commit `a4308e32`. This report was written at `42916d2f`, which has 92 changed Python files between the two (mostly a settings-schema refactor). I re-checked five load-bearing claims at `42916d2f`, and all still hold: the unguarded `int(f["line_idx"])` at [engine_backends/llm_tasks.py:811](/home/user/U00/engine_backends/llm_tasks.py), `max_new_tokens=256` at [asr_backend.py:215](/home/user/U00/asr_backend.py), `CPS_LIMITS` 6.5/8.5 at [subtitle_formats.py:137](/home/user/U00/subtitle_formats.py), the "Below are Chinese source lines" prompts at [llm_tasks.py:575, 753](/home/user/U00/engine_backends/llm_tasks.py), and Fix flagged clearing the flag on any non-empty translation at [services/workspace_job_service.py:614-616](/home/user/U00/services/workspace_job_service.py). I did not re-verify the other audit claims.

## What the strongest CJK pipelines run in October 2026

**Model choice depends on language and domain.** For Mandarin, Cantonese and dialects, Qwen3-ASR-1.7B (Apache 2.0, released 2026-01-29) is the strongest open model on every set its report lists. Examples are WenetSpeech test-meeting at **5.88 vs 19.11 CER** for Whisper-large-v3, Dialog-Mandarin at 6.54 vs 14.01, and ExtremeNoise at 16.17 vs 63.17. All of these are **vendor-reported** by a competitor of the baselines ([Qwen3-ASR report](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.21337)). Japanese has no single winner. On read speech large-v3 and Qwen3-1.7B are roughly even, and a third-party, unverified leaderboard has large-v3 slightly ahead on FLEURS-ja ([Handy ja](https://models.handy.computer/languages/ja)). On in-domain TV speech, kotoba-whisper-v2.0 beats large-v3 (ReazonSpeech **11.6 vs 14.9 CER**), but it loses slightly on read speech ([kotoba-whisper-v2.0 card](https://huggingface.co/kotoba-tech/kotoba-whisper-v2.0)). WhisperJAV, the leading Japanese ASMR tool, ships whisper-ja-1.5B, Qwen3-ASR and anime-whisper. It keeps large-v2 as its classic default because large-v2 is "the most predictable performer on this domain"; that is a **single-source** practitioner judgement ([WhisperJAV](https://github.com/meizhong986/WhisperJAV)). For Korean, Qwen3-1.7B leads large-v3 only slightly, on thin evidence (Q8 FLEURS 4.60 vs 4.89, unverified third party) ([Handy ko](https://models.handy.computer/languages/ko)). VideoLingo has made Qwen3-ASR plus Qwen3-ForcedAligner its default and dropped WhisperX from its installer ([VideoLingo](https://github.com/Huanshere/VideoLingo)).

**Turbo is a speed tier, not a quality default for CJK.** OpenAI says large-v3-turbo (809M parameters, 4 decoder layers) "performs similarly to large-v2" and degrades more on Cantonese and Thai. It cannot translate. A user reports it inserting English words into Japanese output ([openai/whisper #2363](https://github.com/openai/whisper/discussions/2363); [README](https://github.com/openai/whisper)). No primary per-language zh/ja/ko delta between turbo and large-v3 exists. The only quantitative hint is an extrapolation: if turbo performs like v2, kotoba's table shows v2 at **28.1 vs 14.9 CER** against v3 on ReazonSpeech. On consumer GPUs the speed argument for turbo is weak. faster-whisper runs large-v2 (the same size as v3) over 13 minutes of audio in **63 s at 4.5 GB fp16**, or 17 s batched, on an 8 GB RTX 3070 Ti ([faster-whisper](https://github.com/SYSTRAN/faster-whisper)).

**Decoding and silence guards stack in a known order.** The library defaults are a temperature ladder from 0 to 1.0, compression ratio 2.4, logprob −1.0, no_speech 0.6, and `condition_on_previous_text=True` ([whisper transcribe.py](https://github.com/openai/whisper/blob/main/whisper/transcribe.py)). Subtitle pipelines change three things. They force the language. They turn conditioning off, as WhisperX does by default ([whisperX](https://github.com/m-bain/whisperX)) and a Korean broadcast pipeline does ([SceneMaker](https://doc.scenemaker.solbox.com/blog/4/)). And they put a neural VAD in front. In the one controlled study, Silero VAD cut Whisper large-v3's hallucination rate on non-speech from **21.3% to 0.2%**, while WebRTC VAD only reached 12.5%. Adding de-looping and a "bag of hallucinations" post-filter took WER on noise-contaminated speech from 8.0 to **6.5**. That study is English-only ([Barański et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.11378v1)). faster-whisper's own VAD defaults are conservative: it only removes silences over 2 s and pads 400 ms ([vad.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/SYSTRAN/faster-whisper/master/faster_whisper/vad.py)). Subtitle pipelines lower min-silence to 300–500 ms. For whispered or ASMR speech the only guidance is generic (threshold 0.3–0.4), and **no published VAD evaluation on ASMR exists**. `hallucination_silence_threshold` requires word timestamps and scores short words as anomalous ([transcribe.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/openai/whisper/main/whisper/transcribe.py)). That is a plausible reason it hurts fast CJK speech, and it is off by default everywhere checked.

**Vocal separation and denoising are opt-in at best.** Two 2025–26 studies found that speech enhancement before Whisper *raised* WER. In one, the original noisy audio beat enhanced audio in **all 40 configurations** tested ([arXiv 2512.17562](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.17562v1); [arXiv 2603.04710](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04710)). On lyrics, HTDemucs helped Whisper v3 but hurt v2 (search-snippet level) ([arXiv 2408.06370](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.06370)). WhisperJAV keeps enhancement off because "blanket denoising can remove consonant detail" ([WhisperJAV](https://github.com/meizhong986/WhisperJAV)). No study measures separation on conversational CJK drama.

**Alignment means a separate forced aligner and short windows.** Qwen3-ForcedAligner-0.6B reports accumulated average shift of **33 ms (zh), 42 ms (ja) and 37 ms (ko)**, and 36.5 / 81.3 / 42.2 ms on 300 s concatenated audio. On the long-audio test, WhisperX averages 2,708 ms and NeMo NFA 235 ms (zh). These numbers are **vendor-reported** against MFA-labelled references ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-ForcedAligner-0.6B)). WhisperX aligns zh/ja per character with community XLSR-53 models and has no Cantonese default ([alignment.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/m-bain/whisperX/main/whisperx/alignment.py)). stable-ts was archived on 2026-05-30 ([stable-ts](https://github.com/jianfch/stable-ts)), so new work should not depend on it.

**Timing standards are concrete and per language.** These are **primary-sourced** from Netflix:

| Rule | Value |
|---|---|
| Minimum duration | 5/6 s generally; **500 ms in the Japanese guide** |
| Maximum duration | 7 s |
| Minimum gap | 2 frames, with 3–11-frame gaps closed to 2 |
| Lead-out | at least 0.5 s after the audio ends when no subtitle follows |
| zh (Simplified) | 16 CPL, 9 CPS adult |
| ja | 13 CPL horizontal, **4 CPS** |
| ko | 16 CPL, 12 CPS |
| English | 42 CPL, **20 CPS adult / 17 children** |

Sources: [Netflix timing](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/360051554394-Timed-Text-Style-Guide-Subtitle-Timing-Guidelines), [Japanese](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/215767517), [Korean](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/216001127), [Simplified Chinese](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/215986007), [English](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/217350977-English-USA-Timed-Text-Style-Guide). One research note gave Japanese as 16 CPL / 9 CPS and Korean as 42 / 17. Those figures came from a mislabelled article (215986007 is the Chinese guide) and a flagged mis-extraction, so this report uses the values above.

**Translation gains come from memory and context, not bigger models or thinking.** In SMART (a preprint, not peer reviewed, judged by an LLM), removing the series memory of terms and character profiles was the largest single loss (**+0.78** SubMQM penalty). Next were the sliding-window consistency pass (+0.47) and contextual retrieval (+0.56) ([arXiv 2609.38660](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.38660)). One refine pass improves fluency, style and terminology but not adequacy, and a simple refine prompt beat error-specific and evaluate-then-refine schemes ([arXiv 2605.13368](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.13368)). Thinking tokens showed no significant benefit across 10 directions ([arXiv 2510.11919](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.11919)). Open-source tools converge on id- or count-validated batches with retry and recursive splitting, because merged and skipped lines are the documented failure ([TransLora](https://github.com/SloMR/TransLora); [llm-subtrans](https://github.com/machinewrapped/llm-subtrans); [SubtitleEdit #15602](https://github.com/SubtitleEdit/subtitleedit/issues/15602)). For segmentation, **no published comparison shows LLM line-splitting beating rules or an MLM splitter**. The safe design lets a model propose break indices only and verifies that the text is unchanged ([Ponce et al. 2023](https://aclanthology.org/2023.acl-short.67/)). For flagging, LLM and QE span detectors reach only about 17–27 character-level F1 (search-snippet level) ([WMT25](https://aclanthology.org/2025.wmt-1.62.pdf)). In QE4PE, the share of highlighted text was a main driver of *longer* editing time ([QE4PE](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.03044v2)).

## Gaps: what could not be established

Several things could not be established. No independent head-to-head of Qwen3-ASR, Whisper and SenseVoice on real CJK drama or ASMR audio exists. Every Qwen number is vendor-reported, and the repo's only real-audio test uses a non-verbatim reference. No primary per-language zh/ja/ko CER for turbo vs large-v3 was found. No published VAD, aligner or hallucination evaluation covers whispered or ASMR speech, so every ASMR setting here (Baihe's ASMR detector included) is unvalidated. No consumer-GPU VRAM or real-time factor is published for Qwen3-ASR, and the repo has no GPU numbers at all. The research found no quantified evidence that LLM line-splitting beats rules, and no subtitle-specific or CJK-specific precision figures for any flagging signal. The ASR-confidence F1 (~24%) and WMT span-F1 figures rest on search snippets. Some research claims rest on one source or a snippet: the Barański summary of 40.3% non-speech hallucination, the SMART cost appendix (not visible) and its Technical-dimension weight, the HTDemucs lyrics table, the pyvideotrans TEN-VAD default (sources conflict), and the Handy leaderboard. The Baihe audits could not measure real token counts or cache hit rates, the real per-reason flag mix, real line-length distributions, ja/ko behaviour with sudachipy or kiwipiepy installed, or how often real models merge or shift ids. The repo's benchmark scripts and clips are not committed, so none of its numbers can be re-run. Finally, the audits did not establish whether Baihe uses provider batch APIs (a vendor-reported 50% discount) or how it sets reasoning effort for translation.

## Conclusion

The research changes where effort belongs. Baihe's recognition stack is close to what VideoLingo, WhisperX and WhisperJAV ship, and its mixed-language handling is ahead of them. The gains left are in the cheap, deterministic layer that sits between the model and the reviewer: timing rules, calibrated flags, validators. That layer is where the research evidence is strongest (Netflix specifications, validator convergence across tools, QE4PE's finding that noisy flags slow people down), and almost all of it can be tested offline. The expensive LLM passes are the weakest-evidenced part of the pipeline. A paid flag or fix pass is only worth running once the free flags in front of it stop crying wolf.

The one decision that cannot be made from documents is the zh/ja default backend. The vendor's numbers and the repo's single run point in opposite directions. The repo run's hardsub reference systematically penalises verbatim output, and the product path shows signs of truncation and missing line caps that the model itself does not explain. A few hours of verbatim transcription and one GPU run would settle the question, along with large-v3 vs turbo and the aligner window. Until then, the defaults rest on a single-run measurement and a vendor's benchmark that disagree with each other.
